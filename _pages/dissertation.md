 In my dissertation, I report experiments that investigate linguistic interpretation in the domain of legal decision-making, and I build computational models that formally articulate theories of how individuals integrate contextual information when determining the meaning of legal rules.

The first part of my project investigates how lay people (i.e. those who are not legal professionals) interpret rules that express prohibitions or requirements on behavior (e.g. *No electronic devices are allowed in the theater*). Like much of my work within core semantics and pragmatics, this work involves the comparison of computational models that formally articulate the relationship between interpretation and context. In my dissertation, I argue that lay people adjust their interpretations of legal rules according to beliefs about those rules’ policy objectives. (That is, policy context is a relevant feature of legal interpretation). Thus, my research provides evidence that lay people do not interpret rules in the manner prescribed by legal textualists, who tend to discourage the consideration of policy context when reading the law. 

You can learn more about this portion of the dissertation by taking a look at some [recent slides from XPrag 2022](/assets/pdf/xprag_22_slides.pdf) and my recent [poster presented at Sinn und Bedeutung 2022](/assets/pdf/sub_22_poster.pdf).
	
The second part of my dissertation reports an experiment in which lay participants are paired over the internet to collaboratively interpret legal rules. Participants chat with one another before submitting their judgments as to the rules’ meanings. I find that a computational model of legal interpretation that is sensitive to policy context is particularly well-suited to modeling the behavior of pairs who explicitly discuss their beliefs regarding rules’ policy objectives. This analysis not only serves to further validate the model but also suggests that there is substantial variation in the role that lay people believe policy context plays in determining the content of legal rules. In future work, I hope to investigate social factors (including age, gender, and especially political affiliation) that may structure this variability. 

Moreover, I find overall that participants are most likely to discuss rules as they pertain to legal ‘edge cases’ (e.g. the legal status of push scooters with respect to the rule No vehicles are allowed in the town square) and that they negotiate with one another in order to resolve category boundary vagueness (e.g. to resolve what counts as a vehicle for the purposes of the aforementioned rule). From a quantitative analysis of these participants’ discourse strategies, I argue that legal decision-making in this experiment took the form of metalinguistic negotiation, or negotiation over the concepts to which linguistic forms (e.g. vehicle) should map in context. As discussed elsewhere in the philosophy of law literature, this view of legal decision-making problematizes a common assumption about the nature of US-like legal systems, namely that judges do not make laws but merely interpret laws created by an independent legislature. 

Finally, together with [Megan Ma](https://law.stanford.edu/directory/megan-ma/), a postdoctoral researcher at Stanford Law School, I authored a project proposal that has been funded for the 2022-23 academic year by Stanford’s Center for Human-centered Artificial Intelligence. The aim of this ongoing project is to document and model – using a variety of symbolic as well as sub-symbolic computational methods – how interpretation of legal documents varies both as a function of interpretive context and of legal expertise. This project involves collaborations with industry partners (including a global insurance company, an international law firm, and two legal database providers) as well as researchers housed in Stanford’s law, linguistics, and computer science departments.
